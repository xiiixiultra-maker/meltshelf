01  /  What it replaces

The mockup you send a client is a lie by about ten percent. Then the print comes back and everyone is surprised.

Flat mockups warp your artwork with a displacement map. It looks convincing and it is wrong in a specific way: the wrap is stretched to fit a photo rather than mapped to a cylinder. Type near the seam skews, geometric patterns shear, and a circle comes back an ellipse.

This maps to the real thing. The body wrap is 7.194 to 1 because that is the circumference over the label height on the jar it is modelled from. Draw a square, get a square.

02  /  The workflow

Three files in, a jar out.

1

Download the templates

Body wrap, cap skirt and cap top, at the true aspect ratios with the seam, the front centre and the safe area marked. Open them in Illustrator or Photoshop as a guide layer.

2

Drop your artwork in

Drag a PNG onto the surface it belongs to. It maps immediately. Turn the jar, check the seam, check how type sits on the curve, adjust relief and finish.

3

Export

Stills at full resolution, on transparent or on a set background. Send to a client, drop into a deck, or hand to a printer alongside the dieline.

04  /  Brand safety

Unreleased packaging cannot end up on a stranger's shelf.

Meltshelf is also a place where people display jars they own. That raises an obvious question if you are working on packaging that has not launched, so here is the answer.

Studio jars are prototypes, and prototypes are quarantined in the database, not in the interface. They are excluded from public browse and from every count by the projection itself, so no client bug can leak one. They carry a marker rendered on the jar rather than in the surrounding page, which means it survives a screenshot. And they hold no provenance badge, because nobody ever owned them and a badge would be a lie.
